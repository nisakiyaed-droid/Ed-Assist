# The Architecture

> **Document 3 of 4 · The design behind the system · For school leaders and partners**
>
> This document explains the design and reasoning behind our school's lesson-planning system — not just what it does, but why it was built this way.

## The heart of it

We set out to solve one real problem: new teachers struggle to build good lesson plans from scratch. So we built a system where a teacher uploads a textbook chapter, answers five small questions, and receives complete, ready-to-teach session plans — rooted in Indian settings, built around a story that carries children through the whole chapter.

Beneath the convenience sits a deeper intention. Every plan is built the way a master teacher thinks — outcomes first, misconceptions anticipated, learning checked before moving on, every concept wrapped in a story. Over time, using these plans teaches the teacher how to think about teaching. The system is a quiet mentor disguised as a tool.

One principle governs everything: the plan is a preparation document, not a live script. The teacher studies it fully before class, internalises it, and walks in with the lesson living in her.

## What the system involves

Three set-up-once documents — the Framework (which Claude reads), the operating guide, and this Architecture. For every chapter the teacher receives a Chapter Learning Map, Session Plans (each a PDF), Today's Learning Chart inside each, a Chapter Summary, and a complete chapter bundle PDF. Her own input each time is five small questions: chapter number, chapter name, grade, subject, and number of sessions.

## How a chapter unfolds

Claude first confirms it has read the right chapter, recognises a revision unit if that is what was uploaded, and gently suggests a better session count if the number seems off — though the teacher always decides. It then creates the Chapter Learning Map.

Next comes the Session Map — the chapter divided across the chosen sessions, planning the spine of the whole chapter: concepts, pages, vocabulary, a story character who grows across every session, reflection prompts that deepen toward synthesis, and the final session flagged as the Story Finale. Nothing is generated until the teacher approves.

Then, one session at a time and only with permission, Claude builds each plan as its own PDF, opening from the second session with a brief "Chapter Progress So Far" note. After the last session come the Chapter Summary and a complete bundle PDF.

## What a session plan looks like

**Part One — Before Class.** Outcomes, prerequisites, content, reading the story or passage twice, three likely misconceptions, four or five likely questions with ready responses, support and extension, materials, choosing the level, and a short self-check.

**Part Two — During Class.** One clean sequence for the chosen level, with Enhanced leading. No skip rule to manage — she teaches in priority order, always reaching the Story and the Takeaway; anything not reached travels home that evening.

**Part Three — After Class.** Six reflection questions, and the Evening Post ready to paste into Google Classroom.

## The three levels

- **Core** — the six essentials. About twenty-five minutes.
- **Enhanced** — Core plus six enriching elements. About thirty-five minutes. The everyday normal.
- **Full** — adds a genuine cross-curricular connection and a Did You Know. Forty minutes.

The teacher sees only these warm names, never technical labels, and chooses her level while preparing.

## The story — why it is sacred

One character lives across the whole chapter, growing session by session and culminating in the Story Finale — revision disguised as triumph. The story changes shape by subject: narrative for younger grades, a real-world scenario for older, numbers worked through inside a Maths story, a passage with a recurring character for English.

The setting follows the content across India — the Himalayas and the Ganga to the North, the Thar to Rajasthan, the backwaters to Kerala, the South for monsoon and coast — with a gentle South Indian lean when content is neutral. Local colour comes through place, character, food, and festival, always in English, never through inserted regional-language words. Every story ends with one small question left hanging.

## The Evening Post — nothing is lost

At the end of every plan, Claude writes a ready-made Google Classroom post in warm, parent-friendly language, in two layers — a quick glance at the top (the chart, the home task, the curiosity question) and a read-more below (the full story to re-read at bedtime, the new words, the amazing fact). Anything not reached in class is carried here too, which is what makes skipping painless. For children without home access, the teacher uses her own judgment, just as she does with homework.

## What we deliberately left open

How the teacher tells the story, handles a surprising question, manages transitions, and how the coordinator runs the pre-class demo — all left to professional judgment, on purpose. There is intentionally no coordinator document: the demo is a human conversation, and scripting it would diminish it. Structure where new teachers need support; space everywhere their judgment serves better. The plan is the map; the teacher is the journey.

## The intelligence underneath

Claude senses each chapter's type and shifts emphasis without changing structure, checks its own language against the grade level, flags doubtful textbook facts for a human to verify, allows a cross-curricular link only when children genuinely learn in both subjects, keeps the word pool rich, and judges length by content rather than padding. And the system stays alive — teacher reflections and ordinary conversations feed improvements back over time.

Built so every child gets a thoughtfully designed lesson — and every teacher the time to deliver it wonderfully.
